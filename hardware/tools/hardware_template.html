COMP80 Hardware

The PSI COMP80 is one double-sided board, 16 × 12 inches, with the keyboard along the front edge. The Powertran kit put every part on it but the transformer and one power transistor. This page redraws that board from the kit's layout sheet and the circuit diagrams in J. H. Adams's articles, and talks through the circuits section by section.

The processor and its buses

The runs at 2 MHz. It reads and writes through four control lines: RD and WR say which way the data goes, MREQ says it is a memory address and IORQ an input or output port. Most of these signals are active low.

The data bus is heavily loaded, so four 74LS367 buffers stand between it and the Z80, wired as a transceiver: and pass data towards the Z80 when it reads, and away from it when it writes. Their spare gates buffer the control lines.

Two 74LS138 decoders turn port numbers into enables. , enabled by IORQ and WR, picks an output device from A5–A7; , enabled by IORQ and RD, picks an input device from A0–A2. Using different address lines spreads the load on the address bus. The table shows the ports in use, the same numbers the monitor's IN and OUT instructions use.

The keyboard works by interrupt. Its strobe, inverted by , drives the Z80's non-maskable interrupt, so a program that wants a key just executes HALT. If a key arrives when the Z80 is not halted, the monitor's interrupt routine resets the machine, which is how you stop a runaway program.

The clock comes from the display section. The Z80 wants a fast rise to 4.4 V, which a TTL gate can't give, so pulls the clock up actively.

The calculator chip

BURP's arithmetic is done by a National , a scientific calculator on a chip built to work as a small processor of its own. It takes a 6-bit instruction, mostly the code of a calculator key, which the Z80 writes to the latches and with an OUT to port 60.

Two lines pace it. RDY says it is ready for an instruction; HOLD, from the Z80, tells it to wait. The Z80 sets up the instruction, lowers HOLD and waits for RDY. Some instructions make the chip pulse RDY while they run, meant for external counters; the chip's ISEL output marks those moments, and the CMOS gates in use it to hide the pulses from the Z80.

The chip runs from +5 V and −4.5 V, so its HOLD and power-on reset inputs are not TTL levels: and shift them, and its open-drain outputs have pull-down resistors to −4.5 V.

Results come back a digit at a time. When the Z80 asks for the X register, the chip's R/W line pulses twelve times, for the two exponent digits, the signs, the decimal point position and eight mantissa digits. Each pulse interrupts the Z80, which reads the digit through at port 03, along with RDY and the branch line BR that pulses when a test comes out true.

Memory

The base board decodes the bottom 8K. , a 74LS138 enabled when MREQ and A15 are low, splits it into eight 1K blocks from A10–A12, each with its own chip select.

The first three blocks are 2708 EPROMs: the monitor in and BURP in and . The rest is 21L02 static RAM, a 1K × 1 chip, so a kilobyte takes a column of eight, one per bit. Block 0C00 can be either: link 2 gives it to the RAM column , link 1 to a fourth EPROM in (“Fit link 1 or 2 but not both” on the kit sheet). The kit fits three RAM columns; IC24 and IC25 are optional.

A0–A9 go to every memory chip, so and buffer them. The memory chips share their own data bus, joined to the main one by only when the Z80 reads memory. The buffers add delay, but the Z80's tightest reads still leave 750 ns, plenty for 450 ns RAM.

A link in the MREQ line lets the optional 74LS139 split memory into four 8K quarters, for the 32K expansion on the plug .

The display

The display is built from ordinary TTL counters rather than a display controller chip, which was expensive in 1979 and would have made the graphics option harder. Everything runs from the 8 MHz crystal oscillator in , which is also the dot clock: one dot every 125 ns.

and divide it to 4 MHz, the Z80's 2 MHz and 250 kHz. At 250 kHz, steps through sixteen 4 µs states, one television line of 64 µs: state 1 is the line sync, and the picture fills states 4 to 15, 48 µs, leaving margins for a television's overscan.

Within the picture, divides the dot clock by six, one count per character, and and count 64 characters across. counts the ten scan lines of a character row, the last two blank, and with count 32 rows: 320 lines a frame instead of the standard 312½, close enough for a television. After the last row the monostable holds the counters through the frame flyback and makes the frame sync; sets its time.

The screen is 2K of 21L02s, and : six bits of each byte are the character code, and a seventh is there for the graphics option. The multiplexers give the RAM the display counters' address, except while the Z80 writes, when they switch to the Z80's. The Z80 can only write the screen, never read it, and since only A0–A10 and A15 are decoded, the 2K appears sixteen times from 8000 to FFFF.

The character code and the scan line go to the 2513 character generator , which gives five dots. The shift register loads them with a blank sixth for the gap between characters and clocks them out at 8 MHz, through the video gate in to the video pads.

The kit's keyboard

The keyboard

The keys are on the main board itself: 72 switches along the front edge, with a hex keypad on the right and a RESET key that pulls the Z80's reset line straight down. The other keys are scanned by a General Instrument encoder, which drives the matrix columns in turn, watches the rows, and for each key gives a 7-bit code and a strobe. inverts the strobe for the interrupt, and the code is read through at port 01.

The articles don't draw the encoder or the key matrix, so here each key sits at the crossing of the 2376's matrix that produces its code. The kit's real wiring may differ.

The tape interface

Programs are saved on an ordinary cassette recorder at 300 baud, a 0 as about 1200 Hz and a 1 as 2400 Hz. Each byte goes out as a start bit, the eight data bits from bit 7 down, and a stop bit and a half, 35 ms in all.

The Z80 does the timing itself. For each bit it writes D7 to port 20, which clocks one half of the 4013 ; the flip-flop switches a transistor multivibrator between the two tones.

Reading back, a Schmitt trigger squares up the signal from the recorder and the 4528 monostable fires on every cycle. Its pulse is longer than a 2400 Hz cycle but shorter than a 1200 Hz one, so when the other half of IC51 samples it at the next cycle it reads 1 for the high tone and 0 for the low. The Z80 reads the result as D0 of port 00 through , and wants two 0s in a row before it believes a start bit.

Each recording starts with several seconds of stop bits, so there is time to start the tape before typing the READ command.

The power supply

The mains transformer sits off the board with three secondaries. The biggest feeds the 5 V supply, which runs most of the board, including 54 RAM chips: two 1N5401s rectify it into the 4700 µF , and the 7805 regulates it, with driving a 2N3055 on a heatsink off the board to carry most of the current.

A second winding, through a bridge built from four 1N4002s, gives ±12 V from the 7812 and 7912 regulators, and −5 V from a 7905. The EPROMs need +12 V and −5 V, the character generator −5 V and −12 V, the keyboard encoder −12 V. A diode drops −5 V to the −4.5 V the calculator chip uses.

The third winding, through another diode bridge into the 220 µF 100 V , makes a high unregulated rail. Nothing on the main board uses it.

Where it comes from

The placement comes from photos of two sheets that came with the kit; the connections come from the articles' circuit diagrams, transcribed pin by pin.

  • The routed tracks are not the original tracks. They connect the same pins, as the article's circuits do, but their paths were made by an autorouter. Only the RAM columns' buses are laid the way the original artwork lays them.
  • The kit artwork is approximate. The photo resolves about 65 dots per inch, so where tracks run close together they merge.
  • Some pins are a choice. Several figures draw gates and buffers without pin numbers; any gate of the package does the same job. The keyboard matrix is worked out from the encoder's datasheet.
  • Discrete parts sit where the kit sheet shows them when it shows the same reference; the rest go near the pins they connect to. Parts the figures don't number have names like R_CPU3 or TAPE_R7.
