COMP80 v3 Hardware

The v2 board runs the COMP80's ROMs on a Z80 emulator in software. This one builds the computer again in logic, inside an AMD Artix-7 XC7A100T FPGA. There's a Z80 in Verilog with its 64 KB of memory, the I/O ports, the keyboard latch and its NMI, and the display generator, all clocked like hardware. It has v2's outside, HDMI, a USB keyboard, ROMs and tapes on a microSD card, and USB-C power, plus a second USB-C socket that sends the screen to a USB-C monitor as DisplayPort.

Two things stay in software, on a small RISC-V processor inside the same FPGA: the MM57109 calculator chip, which did BCD floating point, and the jobs a modern machine needs, like reading the card and drawing a set-up screen. See the original board, v2, the emulator and the ROM listings.

How it works

Everything in the blue box is logic in the FPGA. The rest are the parts an FPGA needs round it, plus the connectors.

rtl/comp80_machine.v

The COMP80 in logic

The Z80 is TV80, a Verilog Z80 that follows the original's cycle timing, with a clock enable added so it can run inside the FPGA's 50 MHz clock domain. A phase accumulator gives it exactly 2 million T-states a second, the real machine's clock, or 4 million to match MAME and the web emulator.

Around it is the memory map the ROMs expect, the same as the emulators'. That's the ROM and RAM in 0000–1FFF, nothing in 2000–7FFF, and the 2 KB screen at 8000 mirrored up to FFFF, or the 32 KB expansion in its place. The ports decode as the emulators decode them. A key latches for IN 01 and pulls NMI low, as the 1979 keyboard did. The MM57109's interrupt is latched until the Z80 acknowledges it.

The MM57109 is the one part that waits on software. An OUT 60 stalls the Z80 with WAIT until the system controller has passed the instruction to its model of the chip. So an IN 03 straight afterwards reads exactly what the real chip would have shown.

The display

The display keeps its own copy of the screen, written through as the Z80 writes 8000–87FF, so it never has to share the Z80's memory. It draws the 64 × 32 characters in 640 × 480 at 60 Hz, each 6 × 10 cell as 10 × 15 pixels, exactly as v2 does. The two character sets, letters and the 64 graphics blocks, are the MAME driver's, in a block RAM.

Three TMDS encoders turn each pixel into 10-bit symbols, and the Artix-7's serialisers (OSERDESE2, 10:1 at 125 MHz double data rate) send them out on TMDS_33 outputs, straight into the HDMI socket. The pictures are frames from the simulation, made from real COMP80 screens.

DisplayPort over USB-C

The second USB-C socket, , is for a USB-C monitor. It carries DisplayPort in alternate mode: the cable's high-speed pairs carry DisplayPort lanes instead of USB, and its SBU wires carry DisplayPort's AUX channel. The transmitter is Mike Field's open DisplayPort core, made for this Artix-7 family. It sends 800 × 600 at 60 Hz on one lane at 2.7 Gb/s from the FPGA's GTP transceivers, which run from their own 135 MHz oscillator . In place of his test patterns, a COMP80 source draws the same screen as HDMI does, its 6 × 10 characters as 12 × 18 pixels.

Before any of that, the two ends agree terms over USB Power Delivery. The system controller does that through a , a PD interface chip on I²C. It finds the monitor's pull-down on one of the CC pins, which also tells it which way up the plug is, and switches 5 V to the monitor through . Then it asks the monitor what it is and enters DisplayPort mode. Last, it tells the logic which of the socket's four pairs carries the lane and whether the AUX wires are crossed. A USB-C plug fits either way up, and that's how this design copes with it: the FPGA can drive any lane on any pair, so no crossbar switch is needed.

The picture is a frame from the simulation. The COMP80 source and the core's stream-attribute logic ran on a real screen, and the symbols on the lane were decoded back into pixels. All 480,000 match. The transceivers and the AUX channel need hardware to test.

rtl/soc.v, firmware/

The system controller

A PicoRV32, a small RISC-V processor, runs from 128 KB of block RAM loaded with its program when the FPGA configures. It does what the COMP80 never had to: it reads ROM images and tapes from the card, draws the set-up screen, and turns USB keyboard reports into COMP80 key codes.

It also plays the MM57109 and the cassette, with the same C code as v2: mm57109.c, the chip as the MAME driver models it, and tape.c, WAV files played into IN 00 and recorded from OUT 20. Both are timed by the Z80's own T-state count, which the logic keeps. The keys are v2's: F1 for set-up, F2 for tapes, F12 to power on again, Esc for RESET.

The keyboard comes through usb_hid_host, a USB host built entirely in logic. It talks to low-speed keyboards, which most are, without a processor.

The FPGA and its configuration

The comes in a 484-ball BGA at 1 mm pitch; this design uses 226 of the balls. At power-on it loads its configuration from the 16 MB SPI flash in Master SPI mode (M0 high, M1 and M2 low). lights when it's ready. makes it load again, and the JTAG header is for Vivado.

Every I/O bank runs at 3.3 V but bank 35, at 2.5 V for the DisplayPort AUX channel's LVDS. The 100 MHz oscillator feeds a PLL (50 MHz for the system, 12 MHz for USB) and an MMCM (25 MHz pixels, 125 MHz for the serialisers).

Power

The USB-C socket asks for 5 V through its CC resistors. Three TLV62569 buck regulators make the FPGA's rails: for its core and block RAM, for its auxiliary supply, and for the I/O banks and everything else. Each rail has a plane of its own on the inner layers, and a ring of capacitors round the BGA.

Two small LDOs serve the DisplayPort side: for bank 35 and for the transceivers' termination supply. The transceivers' 1.0 V is the core rail through the ferrite bead . Every transceiver supply ball is in the BGA's top-left corner, so their two rails are islands in the inner planes under that corner. Bank 35's 2.5 V is an island too, in the 3.3 V plane under the BGA's left columns.

HDMI, keyboard, card

The HDMI socket is wired straight to four TMDS_33 output pairs in bank 16, which drive the display's 50 Ω terminations themselves. The keyboard's USB-A socket goes to two plain I/O pins with the host's 15 kΩ pull-downs. The microSD socket is in SPI mode, with pull-ups on every data line.

Proving it

No board has been made and Vivado hasn't run yet. Everything else has been checked with open-source tools.

  • The machine against the C model. Verilator runs the logic through LISP, FORTH and BURP test sessions. The screens come out identical to the v2 firmware's C core, which in turn matches the web emulator and MAME. BURP's calculations prove the MM57109 bridge and the WAIT on OUT 60.
  • The whole system boots. With the PicoRV32 running its firmware, the simulation prints COMP80 v3 on the serial port, loads the built-in ROM and starts the Z80. The Z80 reaches the monitor's READY prompt and halts waiting for a key.
  • The display. Icarus Verilog draws frames from real screens (above), and every TMDS symbol decodes back to its pixel.
  • DisplayPort. The COMP80 source's lane symbols, decoded, give back the screen pixel for pixel. The USB-PD firmware runs in the boot simulation as far as reading the FUSB302's ID.
  • It fits. yosys synthesises the whole design for the XC7A100T, all four transceivers included: under a tenth of the logic, and well over half the block RAM still free.

Setting up a board

SETUP.md takes a built board from first power to READY. Check the six rails before programming. Write the bitstream to the flash over JTAG, with Vivado's Hardware Manager or with openFPGALoader, which also runs on macOS. Put the ROMs in /COMP80/ROMS on a FAT32 card and tapes in /COMP80/TAPES.

The guide also covers the keys, the serial console on J6 at 115200 baud, what happens when a USB-C monitor is plugged in, and a table of faults and where to look. The one thing to check first on a new board is the USB-C monitor with the plug both ways up: the lane order for each orientation was worked out from the specifications, not measured.

openFPGALoader -c digilent_hs2 \

What to know

  • Not built, and not through Vivado. The design simulates, synthesises with yosys and fits, and the board passes KiCad's design check. Timing closure and the bitstream are the next step.
  • Assembly. A 484-ball BGA wants a board house that assembles BGAs. The board is six layers, 0.15 mm tracks with 0.12 mm gaps, and 0.45 mm vias with 0.2 mm holes.
  • Low-speed keyboards. usb_hid_host does USB low speed only, and no hubs. Nearly every keyboard is low speed.
  • USB-C video needs a monitor that takes DisplayPort over USB-C and a cable with the high-speed pairs; charge-only cables won't do. The board gives the monitor 5 V but takes no power from it. It's one lane at 800 × 600, enough for the COMP80's 64 × 32 characters.
  • On the silkscreen along the bottom edge: the COMP80's screen from the kit leaflet's cover, dot for dot from the picture program, with the board's name and its line from the 1979 original.
  • Not carried over: the teleprinter port, the floppy disc and real cassettes, as on v2.
The design is in the repository's hardware_v3/ folder. The logic is in rtl/ and the system controller's firmware in firmware/. The simulations are in sim/, and tools/design.py holds the board's parts and connections and writes the pin constraints. This page is made by tools/make_page.py.
